This has happened before, in 2022, in 2012, in 2008. The trigger changes. The structural weakness does not.

Sri Lanka feels every global shock harder than its neighbours, not because the shock is bigger here, but because five structural problems have never been fixed.

How fuel becomes food

You do not need to own a vehicle
for fuel prices to affect you.

Most people in Sri Lanka use public transport or walk. So when fuel prices rise, it is easy to think it does not affect you.

But everything you eat was transported. The fish came on a truck. The vegetables came on a lorry. The rice was milled using energy. The kadé you buy from runs on electricity or kerosene. Every step between the farm and your plate carries a fuel cost.

When that cost goes up, it does not stop at the pump. It moves forward through the chain, and it arrives at your plate within weeks. The data shows this every time.

The Game

Can you spot the hidden fuel penalty?

Every single one. Fuel is inside almost everything. This is why a disruption at a shipping lane in the Middle East ends up in your grocery bill in Colombo.

What triggered this

It started in a narrow stretch
of water between Iran and Oman.

The Strait of Hormuz is 33 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. Roughly 20% of the world's oil passes through it. In February 2026, shipping through the strait became significantly more dangerous. Insurance costs jumped. Several major carriers rerouted. Global oil prices began climbing.

Brent crude moved from approximately $65 per barrel before the disruption to over $117 by April 2026. An 80% increase in roughly two months. Every country that imports oil felt this. But not every country felt it the same way. What happened in Sri Lanka was different in scale, and the reasons go back much further than February 2026.

42%
Diesel rose from Rs. 275 in May 2025 to Rs. 390 in May 2026. A 42% increase in 12 months. The global oil price is the trigger. But it is not the whole explanation.

What is inside the price

Your pump price did not.

In January 2026, Brent was at $65, near its 2020 level. Diesel in Colombo was Rs. 279. By May 2026, Brent had surged to $93 after Hormuz disruptions. Diesel hit Rs. 392. The pass-through was near-instant.

When you pay for a litre of fuel, you are not just paying for the oil. The price at the pump is built from four parts. Understanding what they are is the first step to understanding what can and cannot be controlled.

Does the formula match the pump?

The pricing formula is updated monthly. But the pump price has not always matched it.

↔ Swipe to view full chart

When the formula price is higher than the pump price, fuel is being sold below cost , a hidden subsidy that builds debt. When it is lower, consumers pay above formula. Both happen. Neither is transparent.

Why Sri Lanka always pays more

Same global shock.
Sri Lanka pays far more than
almost every country around it.

Five structural factors explain most of this difference.

Trap 01

A weaker rupee doubles the pain before it arrives.

Sri Lanka buys oil in US dollars. When the rupee weakens against the dollar, every barrel of oil costs more in rupee terms, even if the global price has not moved at all.

In January 2020, the exchange rate averaged Rs. 181 per dollar. By October 2022 it had reached Rs. 365. The rupee had lost more than half its value. Today it sits around Rs. 327 to Rs. 331. A $20 rise in the global oil price now costs a Sri Lankan buyer almost twice as many rupees as it did in 2020.

LKR per USD, 2020–2026.

↔ Swipe to view full chart

The rupee went from Rs. 181 in January 2020 to Rs. 365 by late 2022 , a collapse of 102%. Even after partial recovery to Rs. 291 by end-2024, it sits at Rs. 328 in May 2026.

Trap 02

Sri Lanka holds 30 days of fuel reserves. The minimum recommended is 90.

Countries manage fuel price shocks partly by holding strategic reserves , stockpiles they can draw from when global prices spike, rather than being forced to buy at panic prices in an emergency.

The International Energy Agency recommends a minimum of 90 days of import cover. Sri Lanka typically holds around 30 days. When a shock arrives, Sri Lanka has no option to wait. It must buy immediately, at whatever the international spot price is that day.

A 90-day reserve gives a country the option to wait out a price spike. With 30 days, Sri Lanka is always buying at the worst moment in a crisis , at the peak price, under pressure.

Trap 03

Sri Lanka imports almost all of its fuel. There is no domestic production buffer.

Countries with domestic oil production can partially absorb global price shocks by adjusting how much they export. Sri Lanka has no such cushion. Every litre of fuel used in Sri Lanka must be purchased on international markets, at whatever the spot price is on the day of purchase.

This structural dependency means that a disruption like Hormuz is not filtered or buffered before it reaches the pump. It arrives at full force. The Producer Price Index data for April 2026 shows farm gate prices for agricultural goods fell 15.2% year-on-year, yet consumer food prices rose. The gap is the fuel cost that flows through every step of the chain between the farm and the shelf.

100%
Sri Lanka's dependence on imported petroleum. No domestic production. No export lever. No buffer between the global oil price and your pump price.
Trap 04

Frozen prices do not make costs disappear. They hide them, and pass them to the future.

Between 2020 and 2022, while global oil prices climbed, the government kept Sri Lanka's pump prices frozen. This protected consumers in the short term. But someone had to absorb the growing gap between the formula cost and the price being charged.

That someone was the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation. CPC sold fuel at a loss and borrowed to cover the difference. In early 2022, the formula said diesel should cost Rs. 133 to Rs. 152 per litre. CPC was selling it for Rs. 121. Every litre sold was a loss being recorded in the books.

Rs. 1.7tn
CPC's debt at the peak of the 2022 crisis. The result of years of selling fuel below cost. The debt did not disappear , it was passed on to future consumers through higher prices and to the public through government borrowing.
Trap 05

The food supply chain runs almost entirely on diesel.

There is no significant refrigerated logistics network for fish. There is minimal rail freight for agricultural produce. Small-scale food processing runs on kerosene and diesel generators rather than grid electricity in many areas.

Every one of these gaps means that a fuel price shock transmits more directly and more fully into food prices than it would in a country with better infrastructure. The Producer Price Index shows that while farmgate agricultural prices actually fell 15.2% year-on-year in April 2026 , meaning farmers received less , consumer food prices still rose for many categories. The gap between what farmers received and what consumers paid is partly explained by the transport and energy costs inside the distribution chain.

What everyone is arguing about

Everyone is pointing fingers.
Here is what the data actually supports.

Gov.
The government: This is a global crisis outside Sri Lanka's control.
What the data supports: The Hormuz disruption is a global event. The Singapore Platts price is outside Sri Lanka's control. Market-linked pricing has improved transparency since 2023.

What the data does not support: Blaming only the global situation leaves out the five structural factors that make Sri Lanka's exposure consistently worse than comparable countries.
Opp.
The opposition: This is the current government's fault.
What the data supports: There are legitimate questions about reserve management and whether the 2026 shock could have been better buffered.

What the data also shows: The structural problems existed across multiple governments over more than two decades. Attributing the current crisis to one government alone is not consistent with the historical data.
IMF
The IMF argument: The IMF is forcing price increases and causing hardship.
What the data shows: The 2022 crisis , the queues, the debt, the rupee collapse , occurred partly because the previous system of frozen prices had created unsustainable CPC debt. Whether the IMF programme's conditions are appropriate is a political question. What the data shows is that market-linked pricing has reduced the gap between formula and actual price since 2022.

None of these arguments is entirely wrong. None is entirely right. The full picture requires looking at the data across all of them, which is what this story attempts to do.

The squeeze

Wages fell in real terms.
Food prices doubled.

A price rise is manageable if wages rise at the same rate or faster. The data from the Central Bank of Sri Lanka's Wage Rate Indices shows clearly that they did not.

Between January 2022 and September 2022, the real wage index for public sector workers fell from 91 to 59. Not because anyone had their pay cut. But because inflation , driven significantly by fuel and food prices , destroyed purchasing power faster than wages could adjust.

Real public sector wage index vs food price index, 2020–2026. Both indexed to 2020 baseline. When the lines diverge, purchasing power collapses.

↔ Swipe to view full chart

By September 2022, the real wage index had fallen to 59.1 while the food index had climbed to 210. Recovery has been partial. The gap remains.

35%
In 8 months during 2022, the real purchasing power of a public sector salary fell 35%. The same money bought a third less than it did at the start of that year.

What changed since 2022

Some things are genuinely better.
It is important to say so.

↑
Market-linked pricing was introduced and has broadly worked.

From mid-2023 onwards, pump prices have tracked the formula much more closely than in the 2020–2022 period. This reduces the hidden debt accumulation that caused the 2022 crisis.

↑
CPC's debt has been substantially reduced.

From a peak of approximately Rs. 1.7 trillion in 2022, CPC's debt had been reduced significantly by 2025. This reduces the risk of a repeat of the debt crisis.

↑
The rupee has partially recovered.

From a peak of Rs. 365 in late 2022, the rate recovered to approximately Rs. 291 by end-2024. It is now back around Rs. 327–331 due to the 2026 shock, but this represents a genuine improvement from the crisis peak.

↑
Inflation came down substantially before the 2026 shock.

Food inflation in May 2025 was −0.7% year-on-year , prices were actually falling. The 2026 spike is a new disruption arriving on top of a stabilised base, not a continuation of the 2022 crisis.
