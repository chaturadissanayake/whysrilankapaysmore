Why is everything so expensive again?

A disruption in the Strait of Hormuz pushed global oil prices up 80% in two months. Your basket in Colombo went up 38% in one year. Sri Lanka felt this harder than any country around it. Here is why, and why this keeps happening.

By Chatura Dissanayake · Published June 30, 2026 · 15 - 20 min read

In early 2026, a single disruption pushed global oil prices up 80% in two months. Here in Colombo, your grocery bill went up 38%.

This is not a new story. We saw it in 2022, 2012, and 2008. The trigger changes every time, but the reason Sri Lanka gets hit hardest does not.

Five structural traps make every global shock worse here. A weak rupee. Empty reserves. Total import dependence. Hidden debt. A food chain built entirely on diesel.

There are also five proven ways to reduce the damage. They are not new ideas, and the data to support them already exists.

The short version

If you read nothing else, read this.

The full story and the data behind every number are below. But this is what it comes down to.

Five reasons Sri Lanka pays more
  1. A weak rupee. Sri Lanka buys oil in dollars. The rupee lost more than half its value between 2020 and 2022. Every dollar spent on oil now costs almost twice as many rupees as it did five years ago.
  2. Too little fuel in storage. The recommended minimum is 90 days. Sri Lanka holds around 30. When a shock hits, there is no option to wait, oil must be bought immediately, at whatever the peak price is that day.
  3. 100% imported. Every litre of fuel used in Sri Lanka is bought on international markets. There is no domestic production, no export lever, no buffer between the global price and your pump price.
  4. Hidden debt from frozen prices. Keeping pump prices low in 2020, 2022 felt like relief. But the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation was absorbing the losses. By 2022 that debt had reached Rs. 1.7 trillion. The bill arrived all at once.
  5. A food chain built on diesel. Fish travel by truck. Vegetables come from 200 km away by lorry. Cold storage is minimal. Rail freight is not used. Every fuel price rise passes straight through to food with nothing to absorb it.
Five things that could reduce the damage
  1. Publish the full pricing formula every month. The formula already exists and is updated monthly. Making the full breakdown public would let anyone verify whether the pump price is justified.
  2. Move to weekly price revisions. Monthly changes mean each revision is large and feels like a sudden shock. Countries that revise weekly spread the adjustment across smaller, more predictable steps.
  3. Build reserves toward 90 days. Moving from 30 to 90 days of import cover means Sri Lanka could wait out a price spike instead of buying at the worst possible moment.
  4. Replace blanket subsidies with targeted support. A blanket fuel subsidy delivers most of its value to those who need it least. Direct support to low-income households would protect the people most exposed while costing less overall.
  5. Reduce the food chain's diesel dependence. Cold chain infrastructure, rail freight for agricultural produce, and electrification of small-scale food processing would mean a fuel price rise does not automatically become a food price rise.

None of these are new ideas. All of them are supported by the data. The rest of this story shows you exactly where the numbers come from.

This did not happen by accident.

How fuel becomes food

You do not need to own a vehicle for fuel prices to affect you.

Most people in Sri Lanka use public transport or walk. So when fuel prices rise, it feels like someone else's problem.

But everything you eat was transported. The fish came on a truck. The vegetables came on a lorry. The rice was milled using energy. The kadé you buy from runs on electricity or kerosene. Every step between the farm and your plate carries a fuel cost hidden inside the price.

When that cost goes up, it does not stop at the pump. It moves through the chain and arrives at your plate within weeks. The data shows this every time without exception.

What triggered this

It started in a narrow stretch of water between Iran and Oman.

The Strait of Hormuz is 33 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. Roughly 20% of the world's oil passes through it every day. In February 2026, shipping through the strait became significantly more dangerous. Insurance costs jumped. Several major carriers rerouted. Global oil prices began climbing.

Brent crude moved from approximately $65 per barrel before the disruption to over $117 by April 2026. That is an 80% increase in roughly two months. Every country that imports oil felt this. But not every country felt it the same way. What happened in Sri Lanka was different in scale, and the reasons go back much further than February 2026.

42%
Diesel rose from Rs. 275 in May 2025 to Rs. 390 in May 2026. A 42% increase in 12 months. The global oil price is the trigger. But it is not the whole explanation.

What is inside the price

The global oil price fell. Your pump price did not.

In January 2026, Brent crude was at $65, close to where it was in 2020. Diesel in Colombo was Rs. 279. By May 2026, Brent had surged to $93 after the Hormuz disruption. Diesel hit Rs. 392. The price at the pump followed within weeks.

When you pay for a litre of fuel, you are not just paying for the oil. The price at the pump is built from four components. Two of them, the global oil price and the exchange rate, are outside Sri Lanka's control. The other two are not.

Two components of the pump price are uncontrollable: the Singapore Platts benchmark and the exchange rate. Taxes, processing margin, and admin costs are either fixed or policy choices. Half the price is inside Sri Lanka's control. That half rarely gets discussed.

Does the formula match the pump?

When the formula price is higher than the pump price, fuel is being sold below cost, a hidden subsidy that builds debt. When it is lower, consumers pay above formula. Both have happened. Neither has been communicated clearly to the public.

Why Sri Lanka always pays more

Same global shock. Sri Lanka pays far more than almost every country around it.

Five structural factors explain most of this difference. These are not arguments or opinions. They are in the data.

Trap 01

A weaker rupee doubles the pain before it even arrives.

Sri Lanka buys oil in US dollars. When the rupee weakens against the dollar, every barrel of oil costs more in rupee terms, even if the global price has not moved at all.

In January 2020, one US dollar cost Rs. 181. By October 2022 it cost Rs. 365. The rupee had lost more than half its value. Today it sits around Rs. 327 to Rs. 331. A $20 rise in the global oil price now hits a Sri Lankan buyer with almost double the rupee impact it had five years ago.

The rupee fell from Rs. 181 in January 2020 to Rs. 365 by late 2022, a collapse of 102%. Even after recovering to Rs. 291 by end-2024, it is back at Rs. 328 in May 2026 because of the 2026 shock.

Trap 02

Sri Lanka holds 30 days of fuel reserves. The recommended minimum is 90.

Countries manage fuel price shocks partly by holding strategic reserves, stockpiles they can draw from when global prices spike, so they are not forced to buy at panic prices during a crisis.

The International Energy Agency recommends a minimum of 90 days of import cover. Sri Lanka typically holds around 30. When a shock arrives, there is no option to wait. Oil must be bought immediately, at whatever the international spot price is that day.

A 90-day reserve gives a country the option to wait out a price spike. With 30 days, Sri Lanka is always forced to buy at the worst moment, at the peak price, under pressure, with no alternative.

Trap 03

Sri Lanka imports 100% of its fuel. There is nothing between the global price and your pump price.

Countries with domestic oil production can absorb global price shocks by adjusting how much they export. Sri Lanka has no such option. Every litre of fuel used here must be purchased on international markets at whatever the spot price is that day.

This means a disruption like Hormuz is not filtered or buffered before it reaches the pump. It arrives at full force. The Producer Price Index data for April 2026 shows farmgate prices for agricultural goods fell 15.2% year-on-year, farmers received less, yet consumer food prices still rose. That gap is the fuel cost flowing through every step between the farm and the shelf.

100%
Sri Lanka's dependence on imported petroleum. No domestic production. No export lever. No buffer between the global oil price and the price at your pump.
Trap 04

Freezing prices does not make costs disappear. It hides them and passes them to the future.

Between 2020 and 2022, while global oil prices climbed, the government kept Sri Lanka's pump prices frozen. This felt like relief. But someone had to absorb the growing gap between the formula cost and the price being charged at the pump.

That someone was the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation. CPC sold fuel at a loss and borrowed to cover the difference. In early 2022, the formula said diesel should cost Rs. 133 to Rs. 152 per litre. CPC was selling it for Rs. 121. Every litre sold was a loss recorded in the books. By mid-2022, the bill became impossible to ignore.

Rs. 1.7tn
CPC's debt at the peak of the 2022 crisis. Years of selling fuel below cost produced this. The debt did not disappear, it was passed to future consumers through higher prices, and to the public through government borrowing.
Trap 05

The food supply chain runs almost entirely on diesel. There is no alternative route.

There is no significant refrigerated logistics network for fish. There is minimal rail freight for agricultural produce. Small-scale food processing in many areas runs on kerosene and diesel generators rather than the grid.

Every one of these gaps means that a fuel price shock passes more directly and more fully into food prices than it would in a country with better infrastructure. When farmgate prices actually fell 15.2% year-on-year in April 2026, meaning farmers received less, consumer food prices still rose for many categories. The gap between what farmers received and what consumers paid is the transport and energy cost inside the distribution chain.

What everyone is arguing about

Everyone is pointing fingers. Here is what the data actually supports.

Gov.
The government: This is a global crisis outside Sri Lanka's control.
What the data supports: The Hormuz disruption is a global event. The Singapore Platts price is outside Sri Lanka's control. Market-linked pricing since 2023 has improved transparency.

What the data does not support: Blaming only the global situation leaves out the five structural factors that make Sri Lanka's exposure consistently worse than comparable countries. Those factors predate this government.
Opp.
The opposition: This is the current government's fault.
What the data supports: There are legitimate questions about reserve management and whether the 2026 shock could have been better buffered with more preparation.

What the data also shows: The structural problems existed across multiple governments over more than two decades. Attributing the current crisis to one government alone is not consistent with the historical record.
IMF
The IMF argument: Conditionality is forcing price increases and causing hardship.
What the data shows: The 2022 crisis, the queues, the debt, the rupee collapse, occurred partly because frozen prices had built unsustainable CPC debt over years. Whether the IMF programme's conditions are appropriate is a political question. What the data shows is that market-linked pricing has reduced the gap between formula and actual price since 2022.

None of these arguments is entirely wrong. None is entirely right. The full picture requires looking at all of them at once, which is what this story attempts to do.

The squeeze

Wages fell in real terms. Food prices doubled.

A price rise is manageable if wages rise at the same rate or faster. The data from the Central Bank of Sri Lanka's Wage Rate Indices shows clearly that they did not.

Between January 2022 and September 2022, the real wage index for public sector workers fell from 91 to 59. Not because anyone had their pay cut. Because inflation, driven significantly by fuel and food prices, destroyed purchasing power faster than wages could possibly adjust.

By September 2022, the real wage index had fallen to 59.1 while the food index had climbed to 210. Recovery has been partial. The gap remains.

35%
In 8 months during 2022, the real purchasing power of a public sector salary fell by 35%. The same salary bought a third less than it did at the start of the year. No pay cut was announced. It happened through prices.

What changed since 2022

Some things are genuinely better. It matters to say so.

↑
Market-linked pricing was introduced and has broadly worked.

From mid-2023 onwards, pump prices have tracked the formula much more closely than in the 2020, 2022 period. This reduces the hidden debt accumulation that caused the 2022 crisis.

↑
CPC's debt has been substantially reduced.

From a peak of approximately Rs. 1.7 trillion in 2022, CPC's debt had been reduced significantly by 2025. This lowers the risk of the same debt crisis recurring.

↑
The rupee has partially recovered.

From a peak of Rs. 365 in late 2022, the rate recovered to approximately Rs. 291 by end-2024. It is now back around Rs. 327, 331 due to the 2026 shock, but this is a genuine improvement from the crisis peak.

↑
Inflation came down substantially before the 2026 shock.

Food inflation in May 2025 was −0.7% year-on-year, prices were actually falling. The 2026 spike is a new disruption arriving on top of a stabilised base, not a continuation of the 2022 crisis.
